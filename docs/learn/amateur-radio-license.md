# So, you want to get your ham radio license?

Good! And before you decide that this sounds like something only electrical engineers can do, let me tell you a little about what's actually involved.

You don't need an engineering degree, a room full of expensive radios, or the ability to understand Morse code. You don't even need to own a radio to take the exam.

This guide is about getting licensed in the United States. If you live somewhere else, check with your country's regulator. The rules and exams aren't necessarily the same.

## Can I do anything before I get licensed?

Absolutely. In the US, listening to ordinary amateur radio transmissions doesn't require an amateur license. You can use a receive-only SDR, listen to local repeaters, decode APRS packets, or explore ADS-B. You can learn a ton that way.

Transmitting on amateur frequencies on your own is a different matter. Get your license first, stay within your privileges, and learn the operating rules. An internet-only demo is not permission to turn on a transmitter.

## Which license do I need?

There are three US license classes: Technician, General, and Amateur Extra.

Start with Technician. It gives you access to the amateur VHF/UHF bands, including the 2-meter band where a lot of APRS activity happens, plus limited HF privileges. General and Extra expand your operating privileges, especially on HF. You don't have to earn all three to have a perfectly enjoyable time in the hobby.

The Technician exam has 35 multiple-choice questions. You need 26 correct to pass. No Morse code test. The license lasts ten years before renewal.

## How I'd approach studying

Start with [HamStudy](https://hamstudy.org/). The web study tools are free. Work through the questions a little at a time, read the explanations when something doesn't make sense, and take practice exams.

As of November 2026, the Technician question pool is the July 2026–June 2030 pool. Make sure whatever book or app you use is preparing you for the questions in effect on your exam date. The [ARRL question pool page](https://www.arrl.org/question-pools) is a good place to check.

If you like a book that explains the material instead of just drilling questions, the [ARRL Ham Radio License Manual](https://www.arrl.org/ham-radio-license-manual) is another option. A local radio club can also be a great source of help. Ask questions. Most of us got into this hobby because we like figuring things out.

My suggestion is to aim for consistently passing practice exams with a comfortable margin. Don't worry about mastering every bit of electronics theory before you take the test. Getting your license is the beginning of learning, not the end of it.

## Taking the exam

1. Register for a free FCC Registration Number (FRN) through [FCC CORES](https://apps.fcc.gov/cores/userLogin.do). Keep your login details private.
2. Find a volunteer examiner team through [HamStudy's exam listings](https://hamstudy.org/sessions) or [ARRL's getting licensed page](https://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed). Online and in-person sessions are available.
3. Follow that team's instructions for identification, FRN, payment, and any online testing requirements. Exam fees vary by team; some are free.
4. Pass the exam. The exam team submits your application, and the FCC sends payment instructions. The new-license application fee is currently $35, separate from any exam fee. See [ARRL's fee information](https://www.arrl.org/fcc-application-fee).
5. Wait until your license and callsign are granted in the [FCC Universal Licensing System](https://wireless2.fcc.gov/UlsSearch/searchLicense.jsp) before operating your own transmitter.

One thing worth knowing: amateur license records are public, including the mailing address on the license. Consider a lawful mailing address you're comfortable having public before applying.

## I have my callsign! Now what?

You don't need to rush out and spend hundreds of dollars on equipment. Find a local club or repeater, listen to how people operate, and introduce yourself when you're ready. Learn about station identification, frequency privileges, antenna placement, and how to avoid interfering with other stations.

For our corner of the hobby, try [receiving APRS](aprs.md), then learn about [iGates](igate.md). Keep transmission disabled until you've checked your station setup and understand the local channel.
